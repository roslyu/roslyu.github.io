brain smoothing & the cost of narrow work
On how repetitive work flattens time and creativity — and how I started rebuilding texture.
read more →

There's a phenomenon I call "brain smoothing" — when days blend together, novelty fades, and different versions of yourself become harder to access. It happens when work becomes too narrow, too repetitive, too flattened.

I observed this firsthand in public accounting: the same screens, the same rhythms, the slow erosion of creative curiosity. Reading for hours, wandering, noticing things just because, and having a chuckle with your peers about different topics. All of it became harder.

The year after PwC was an experiment in rebuilding texture. The website. The macarons. The dogs. The woodworking. The solo travel. As evidence: curiosity can be reclaimed. Seasons of yourself can return. You just have to make space for them.

big firm, big trade‑off
Why I chose a large firm, what it gave me, and why I knew when to step away.
read more →

I chose PwC because it was a big firm. That meant pools of peers joining at the same time, global connections, the ability to transfer offices, and a launchpad for opportunities I wouldn't have known how to find on my own.

There's a real upside to that kind of environment, especially in your twenties. Being inside a large organization with good benefits and a global reach can feel like the fastest way to get somewhere. And honestly, I loved my team. The people made it worthwhile.

But there's a trade‑off. The same structure that opens doors also narrows your days. High expectations, long hours, and the slow erosion of personal projects and unstructured freedom. I don't regret it — I needed the launchpad. But I also needed to know when to step away.

When you're always being told what to do — when someone else maps out the path detail by detail — you don't have space to think critically or look beyond the microscope. It's hard to see that there are options. Hard to be creatively minded. That was the hardest part: trusting myself to make a decision. Not because anything was broken, but because I had gotten what I needed and it was time to build my own map.

Building your own map is the best learning experience. You have the freedom to explore, to pivot, to create — without too many commitments crowding your life and taking away the space you need to think.

A close friend and former colleague at PwC left a year after I did. When she finally chose to step away from a situation she had outgrown, she said something that stuck with me:

"I came to the conclusion that there are no good decisions, no bad decisions — decisions are just decisions."

That's how I think about it now. You make a decision, and then life evolves from that point. That's how you learn. Decisions carry consequences — sometimes positive, sometimes negative — and that's how you grow, if you have the courage to make them in the first place.

LGBTQ+ inclusion in state accountancy boards
Research from my master's program on how state laws address (or don't address) LGBTQ+ inclusion in professional licensing and public accommodations.
read more →

During my graduate research assistantship, I studied state accountancy board laws to analyze whether LGBTQ+ individuals were explicitly included in public accommodations and accessibility language. The findings varied significantly by state, some explicitly inclusive, some silent, some ambiguous.

The research reinforced my interest in how professional regulation intersects with civil rights, and how vague language can create real barriers.

One of the main takeaways from my research on LGBTQ+ language in state regulatory codes was that explicit protections in public accommodation laws carry immense weight. They show that even long-established, powerful boards recognize the need to change and improve.

What I researched: I assisted a professor by analyzing statutes and accountancy board laws across the fifty states and their anti-discrimination policies. Specifically, I looked for:

  • General references to diversity and equity issues
  • Specific references to discrimination and "protected classes"
  • References to discrimination related to clients (public accommodations)
  • Exceptions, limitations, and private right of action
  • Whether the codes explicitly enumerated sexual orientation or gender identity

The paradox of the profession: Public accountants are held to a strict code of ethics. Acts deemed "discreditable" are taken seriously, or so the rules state. In practice, the story may be a little different (I have a lot to share about public accounting in general). The work itself is narrow. Working in a large public firm, surrounded by other CPAs, puts you in a bubble, shielded from real-world communities, from inspiration, from creativity. Although the rapid professional development is invaluable, identity and outlook can easily become lost. A core purpose of this research was to see how different states are adapting to this and whether they are trying to broaden the bubble.

The specific question of client refusal: Because accountants serve the public, I focused on whether anti-discriminatory language appears in legal codes regarding clientele (public accommodations). Generally, a place of business cannot refuse service to someone in a protected class. But how much legal right does a public practice accountant have to turn away a client? That is harder to determine.

What else I examined: I also researched whether state licensing boards are acknowledging these issues, whether they are growing with social trends, and recognizing DEI principles. I looked at the AICPA Code of Professional Conduct, as well as the CIMA/CGMA and NAEA codes of ethics.

Why this matters to me: During my Master's program, we discussed accounting as a declining profession. Traditional requirements for rigor and success no longer align with what newer generations (myself included) value: authentic work, work-life balance, and genuine connection. Workplaces that speak to who they are. Work that feels genuinely fulfilling, even the small bits. This research felt like a small part of asking whether the profession can change.

culture & travel — observing how place shapes perception
Notes on cultural relativity, the geography of reality, and what I learn from being somewhere new.
read more →

I love traveling not just to see new places, but to notice how people in different environments perceive reality differently. Growing up somewhere shapes what you assume is normal, what you value, how you move through the world.

Cultural relativity isn't just an academic concept; it's something you feel when you're sitting in a café in a country where the pace of life is slower, or walking through a market where haggling is expected, or driving on the opposite side of the road and realizing how much of what you know is just habit.

These observations don't have neat conclusions. I'm collecting them to remind myself that my way of seeing is not the only way. Perhaps this is why so many professionals find themselves on "spiritual journeys" in much simpler spaces. The spaces serve as an escape from issues with their reality. More reflections from the road.
